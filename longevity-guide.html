03 — Daily rhythm

What a good day actually looks like

Longevity is built in daily habits, not annual checkups. This is a research-informed daily template — not a rigid plan, a direction.

6:00–7:00 AM
Wake with the light. No phone for 30 minutes.
Morning light sets your circadian rhythm — which controls cortisol, insulin, and sleep quality. Delaying phone use lowers morning cortisol spike.
↳ Circadian biology / sleep science
7:00–8:00 AM
High protein breakfast. No skipping.
Skipping breakfast worsens glucose control for the rest of the day. Aim for eggs, curd, or dal — protein slows glucose absorption and creates satiety.
↳ Meal timing research + ICMR-INDIAB dietary data
After every meal
Walk for 10 minutes.
Post-meal walking is one of the most effective ways to lower postprandial glucose. Even 10 minutes significantly reduces the glucose spike. No gym required.
↳ Postprandial glucose research — multiple clinical trials
12:00–1:00 PM
Lunch: rice with dal and sabzi — in that pairing.
Eat vegetables first, then dal, then rice. Order matters — fiber and protein first slows the glucose impact of carbohydrates eaten after.
↳ Mohan et al. 2014 rice substitution RCT
4:00–5:00 PM
Almonds as a snack. Not biscuits.
43g of almonds daily reduced insulin resistance in overweight Asian Indians over 12 weeks. Replace the afternoon biscuit or namkeen habit with a small handful.
↳ Gayathri et al. Frontiers in Nutrition 2023
6:00–7:00 PM
Strength or resistance training — 2–3x per week.
Muscle mass is a longevity biomarker. South Asians tend to have lower muscle mass (sarcopenia) which worsens insulin resistance. Building muscle is one of the most impactful things you can do.
↳ South Asian phenotype research — Mohan 2025
7:00–8:00 PM
Light early dinner. Done by 8pm if possible.
Eating late disrupts circadian metabolism. The body handles glucose better earlier in the day. A 12-hour eating window (8am–8pm) is a simple form of time-restricted eating with solid evidence.
↳ Chrono-nutrition and time-restricted eating research
10:00–10:30 PM
Sleep. Same time every night.
Consistency matters more than duration. Going to bed at the same time trains your circadian rhythm, improves sleep quality, and normalizes cortisol and insulin the next morning.
↳ Sleep and metabolic health research

Where this knowledge comes from

Every recommendation in this guide is grounded in peer-reviewed research. These are the key papers — not summaries, not blog posts.

Br J Nutr · 2009 · CURES-59 · n=1,843
Dietary carbohydrates, glycaemic load and newly detected type 2 diabetes in urban Chennai
Refined grains: OR 5.31 higher T2D risk. Fiber: OR 0.31 protective. White rice = 66% of total carb intake.
Mohan V et al. → doi.org/10.1017/s0007114509990468
Nature Medicine · 2025 · ICMR-INDIAB · n=18,090
Dietary profiles and metabolic risk factors across India
High rice intake: 19–26% higher T2D/prediabetes odds. High added sugar: 14–26% higher odds. National survey data.
Anjana RM, Mohan V et al. → doi.org/10.1038/s41591-025-03949-4
Diabetes Technol Ther · 2014 · RCT · n=15
Brown rice vs white rice: glucose and insulin responses in overweight Asian Indians
Brown rice reduced glucose AUC by 19.8%, fasting insulin by 57% vs white rice over 5 days.
Mohan V et al. → doi.org/10.1089/dia.2013.0259
Frontiers in Nutrition · 2023 · RCT · n=400

43g/day almonds for 12 weeks: HOMA-IR −0.4, insulin disposition index +0.7, cholesterol −5.4 mg/dL.
Gayathri R, Mohan V et al. → doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2022.1055923
Diabetes Care · 2025 · Kelly West Lecture
